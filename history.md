## The Technion Philadelphia Flight Control Laboratory Anno 1971

This is the story of the Flight Control Laboratory at the Faculty of Aerospace Engineering of the Technion. It is a tribute to the late **Professor Shmuel Merhav**, who started the laboratory in 1971 from scratch, and whose vision led to developments that were far ahead of their time. 

Today the Laboratory is a thriving, exciting place on the forefront of autonomous navigation, perception, and cooperative autonomous systems. Many techniques and systems that are commonly used today are rooted in a long evolutionary process that took place through the 50+ years of existence of the laboratory. Today’s students and researchers take tools like MATLAB, Simulink, LabVIEW, Mathematica, SolidWorks, and 3D printing for granted. However, none of these tools existed when the lab was founded, and inventiveness, creativity, and vision were required to carry out flight control research under stringent budgetary restrictions.

---

## How It Started

In September 1971, **Professor Shmuel Merhav** left RAFAEL (the Israeli Armament Development Authority) to start a new Flight Control Laboratory at the Faculty of Aerospace Engineering. For this purpose, the Faculty dedicated a dark and dusty space, adjacent to the transonic wind tunnel, located at the Aeronautical Research Center. The place was fenced off from the rest of the complex by a two-meter-high corrugated plastic fence. Apart from two work benches, a table, and some chairs, the place was empty. The laboratory equipment consisted of one oscilloscope, a few soldering irons, and a small mechanical workbench. The laboratory staff consisted of chief electronic engineer Yeheskel Ben-Arie and two technicians, Elhanan Rabinovitch and Benni Cooper.

**Professor Itzhack Bar-Itzhack** joined the Faculty at the same time, with research interests in Inertial Guidance and Navigation Systems. Two Ph.D. students joined Professor Merhav in his efforts to build the laboratory. The focus of interest was research on pilot-vehicle systems, flight control, and aerospace guidance and navigation.

The laboratory owes its name to the **Philadelphia Chapter of the American Technion Society (ATS)**, whose members adopted the Laboratory 18 years later, in the year 1989. The Chapter contributed a large part of the computational equipment and electronics in the laboratory, which proved imperative in a wide range of research activities for years to come.

In 1971, the laboratory’s first breakthrough was the acquisition of a used **EAI analog computer**, donated by the Faculty of Electrical Engineering. This computer consisted of operational amplifiers wired to solve sets of differential equations in real time. Digital real-time simulation was impossible at that time without a fully devoted CDC Cyber computer or IBM mainframe. The analog computer was essential in simulating the dynamic response of aircraft in real time. Programming took place by plugging colorful wires into a removable patch board.

## The First Visual Flight Simulators

The first laboratory efforts were devoted to building a flight simulator. Computer graphics did not yet exist, and images had to be generated by optical systems and servo drive hardware. The first system was based on the "shadowgraph" principle, consisting of a large circular transparent Perspex disk containing the outline of a visual scene, projected onto a large screen by a halogen point-source lamp.

In the early 1970s, Remotely Piloted Vehicles (RPVs) were in their infancy. Early on, Professor Merhav saw the potential of RPVs and started research projects directed at effective manual control using vehicle-based cameras. A 5-degrees-of-freedom RPV simulator was constructed from a discarded viewgraph projector fitted with servo motors, looped transparency belts with dotted landscape patterns, and a vidicon camera.

Landing an RPV via the vehicle camera proved extremely challenging without aids. Predictive symbols generated on a separate stroke-written CRT were overlaid on the scene. During a visit of Apollo astronauts to the Technion, after flying the simulator they remarked that the dotted landscape reminded them of the moon landing. Other notable visitors flying the simulator included Egyptian and Israeli Ministers of Defence, Ali and Weizman, during the historic 1979 visit of President Anwar Sadat.

## The First Building & The First Digital Computer

In 1973, the laboratory moved to a dedicated prefab building near the Asher Space Research Institute site, receiving a second EAI analog computer. 

In 1974, the laboratory received its first digital computer: a **Data General Nova 2** with 32 KB of memory and a 16-bit word length, programmed in assembly language via punched paper tape. With an analog-to-digital converter and digital tape drive, recording and post-processing experimental data replaced manual brush recorders, enabling spectral density distribution and recursive parameter estimation.

## Pilot-Vehicle Modelling & Academic Curriculum

Human operator modelling formed a major research pillar, bridging McRuer’s "Cross-Over Model" and linear quadratic optimal estimation models from BBN. Professor Merhav and Professor Bar-Itzhack introduced fundamental courses that shaped aerospace education:

* **Automatic Flight Control:** Pioneered by Prof. Merhav, based on classical control and state-space methods.
* **Aerospace Sensor Systems:** Authored by Prof. Merhav in textbook form, establishing complementary filtering concepts.
* **Inertial Navigation:** Authored by Prof. Bar-Itzhack, teaching gyro theory, coordinate transformations, and inertial platforms.
* **Vibrating Coriolis Angular Rate Sensor:** Pioneered by Prof. Merhav and Elhanan Rabinowitz using accelerometers on a vibrating arm to measure Earth rotational rates—a forerunner to modern MEMS gyroscopes.

---

## Image Processing, Stroke Graphics & Tunnel-in-the-Sky

In the mid-1970s, Prof. Merhav and Chief Engineer Yeheskel Ben-Arie introduced image processing in aerospace applications with a Vicom Digital Image Processor and a high-precision 4-axis light table for terrain-aided navigation and motion detection.

A Data General Eclipse computer was acquired (now exhibited at the Madatech National Science Museum in Haifa). Stroke-written computer graphics enabled the pioneering **"Tunnel-in-the-Sky"** display research—presenting 3D winding and descending flight paths with predictor symbols to pilots, which later evolved into modern cockpit Head-Up Displays (HUD) and Synthetic Vision Systems (SVS).

---

## Moving Base Simulators & The Lady Davis Era

In the early 1980s, the laboratory moved to its permanent home in the **Lady Davis Building**.

* **3-DOF "Yoyo" Simulator:** Designed in-house with a suspended cabin driven by servo rods to study suppression of biodynamic interference in buffeting flight using adaptive noise cancellation, in collaboration with the NASA Ames Research Center.
* **6-DOF Stewart Platform:** Built in 1985 with support from Mr. Steve Meadow, providing a statically defined motion base driven by linear torque motor cable drives.
* **High-Accuracy 3-Axis Moked Flight Table:** Specified by Prof. Bar-Itzhack for strap-down navigation, Kalman filter sensor fusion, and hardware-in-the-loop simulation.

## The Modern Era: Robotics, Autonomous Navigation & AI

Through the 1990s and 2000s, PFCL transitioned from Silicon Graphics pictorial workstations and cyclorotor hovering testbeds to autonomous robotic systems.

Today, PFCL stands at the forefront of autonomous aerospace systems, uniting research groups in:

* **Autonomous Navigation and Perception Lab (ANPL):** SLAM, active perception, multi-robot coordination.
* **Cooperative Autonomous Systems Lab (CASY):** Differential games, cooperative guidance, interception.
* **Cooperative Networks and Controls Lab (ConNeCt):** Graph-theoretic network control, swarm dynamics.
* **Stochastic Estimation & Control (Idan Group, Ben-Asher Group, Oshman Group):** Advanced filtering, calculus of variations, and fault detection.
* **Teaching Laboratories:** Educating generations of Technion engineers with hands-on drone flight, rotary pendulums, and capstone aerospace systems.
